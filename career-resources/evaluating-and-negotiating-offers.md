# Evaluating And Negotiating Offers

A structured way to assess a role, compare offers, and negotiate without inventing leverage.

[Back to career resources](README.md)

## Information To Collect

Evaluate more than the headline compensation:

- role, level, decision authority, and expected outcomes;
- manager, team structure, onboarding, and feedback practices;
- product or project maturity and the work expected in the first six months;
- employment entity, client relationship, and payroll arrangement;
- work location, remote or hybrid expectations, travel, shifts, and working days;
- fixed pay, variable pay, equity, joining or retention payments, and repayment clauses;
- insurance, leave, retirement benefits, allowances, and learning support;
- probation, notice period, termination terms, and background checks; and
- growth criteria, review cycle, and the evidence used for promotion.

Ask the recruiter which terms will appear in writing. Resolve conflicting verbal statements before
accepting.

## Questions For The Hiring Manager

- What outcome would make the first six months successful?
- Which problems will this role own, and which decisions require approval?
- What is the current team composition and on-call or operational responsibility?
- Why is the position open?
- Which technical or organizational constraint is currently slowing the team down?
- How are priorities changed when capacity is insufficient?
- How are performance and promotion decisions made?
- What does onboarding provide, and where must a new hire create missing context?

Prefer concrete examples over assurances such as “great culture” or “unlimited growth.”

## Independent Due Diligence

Use public, lawful sources to verify the legal employer, product, leadership, funding or financial
position where relevant, recent restructuring, and employee tenure patterns. Speak with current or
former employees when appropriate, while respecting their confidentiality.

Useful questions include:

- Did the day-to-day role match what was promised?
- How were priorities, incidents, and performance concerns handled?
- What caused strong employees to succeed or leave?
- Were working hours and location expectations predictable?

One person's experience is evidence, not a complete verdict. Look for consistent patterns and date
each observation.

## Comparing Total Compensation

Normalize offers to the same time period and distinguish guaranteed from conditional value.

```text
guaranteed cash
+ realistically earned variable pay
+ employer-funded benefits you value
+ risk-adjusted equity or long-term incentives
- one-time or recurring costs created by the role
- repayments or forfeitures triggered by leaving
```

Do not count an advertised maximum bonus as guaranteed. For equity, understand the instrument,
vesting schedule, exercise terms, liquidity, dilution, tax treatment, and what happens when
employment ends.

## Negotiating

State the evidence and request clearly:

```text
Based on the role's [scope or level], the market information I reviewed, and my relevant experience
with [specific evidence], I am targeting [range or term]. I am interested in the role and would like
to understand whether there is flexibility in [base pay, level, joining date, equity, or another
specific term].
```

Prioritize the few changes that materially affect the decision. Do not invent another offer, reveal
another organization's confidential terms, or threaten to leave after accepting. If circumstances
change after acceptance, communicate promptly and accept that the employer may withdraw the offer.

## Decision Record

Before accepting, write down:

- essential requirements and deal-breakers;
- the evidence supporting each assumption;
- unresolved risks and who owns them;
- the complete written terms;
- the acceptance deadline; and
- why this choice best supports the next two or three years.

This prevents a late salary difference or a persuasive conversation from hiding a larger mismatch
in role, manager, location, stability, or growth.
